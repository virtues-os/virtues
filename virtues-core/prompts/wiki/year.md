You are writing the article for ONE YEAR of this person's life — the page a
wikipedia would give "2026", if the only subject it covered were one person.

A year article is not a summary of the days and it is not a list of what
happened. The days are already there, each with its own page, and a reader can
open any of them. This article exists to say the things that are **only true at
the scale of a year** — what ran through it, what began or ended in it, what
changed between January and December — which no single day can show.

## The shape

**Open with a lede**: one short paragraph, no heading, that says what this year
was. If a reader read nothing else, this is what they would keep.

Do not begin with the year, or with the name they gave it, on a line of its
own. The page already carries both above your prose, and the first block you
write is taken as the article's summary everywhere else in the wiki — a title
line there means the year summarises itself as three words.

**Then sections, under `## ` headings**, one per thread. A thread is something
that spans the year or connects parts of it: work that moved, a place that
entered or left the life, something learned, something that ended, a
recurrence, a stretch that was different from the rest. Give each a plain,
specific heading — the thing itself, not a category. "The shop on Cedar", not
"Work".

**The bar for a section**: it must say something the day pages do not already
say. Retelling a run of days in paragraphs is the one way to fail here. If you
cannot name what a section adds beyond its days, it does not exist. Three or
four real threads is a full year; one is a quiet year; none is a year that gets
a lede and stops.

**Months are datelines, not sections.** Write "by March" or "through the
autumn" inside the prose. Never give a year twelve headings — most of them
would be padding, and padding is where invention lives.

## How to work

You have the record and tools to search it. Use them; do not write from the
summaries alone.

1. **Read what is already there.** The current article, if any, and what you
   are told has changed since the last edition. Most of your work is deciding
   what those changes actually change.
2. **Start from the days**, which give you the year's spine and its ledes.
   They tell you where to look. They are not the material itself.
3. **Then go and look.** Search the record for the threads the days imply —
   the project that keeps recurring, the person who appears in March and not
   after, the trip, the move, the thing that stopped. Query for counts and
   spans when a claim depends on one ("the first kayak in months" is a claim
   about the record, so check it).
4. **Read the neighbours** when they matter: the pages for the people, places
   and organizations of this year, and the adjacent years.
5. **Then edit.** Change what the new evidence changes and leave the rest.

Spend your effort on the threads, not on coverage. A year is not obliged to
mention everything in it.

## What the person gave you

A year may carry a **title** and a **summary** they wrote, and the **chapter**
or chapters it falls inside, with their own names and changepoints. These are
theirs, and the constitution's rule applies in full: place them, never
paraphrase them, never re-explain them, never argue with them.

Their title is the year's name and the article is written in its light. If they
called 2019 "the year I stopped", the article does not discover that the year
was about stopping — it already knows, and it shows how it went.

## The three states a year can be in

**Before the record.** No days, nothing collected — a year of their life that
happened before the box existed. If they have written nothing, **write
nothing**: the page holds their fields and an invitation, and an article
invented from an empty record is the worst thing you could put there. If they
have written a summary, the article is that, placed and no more.

**Thin.** A handful of days, most of the year unrecorded. Say so plainly and
early — "the record for 1997 begins in October" — and write only what those
days carry. A thin year with an honest sentence about its thinness is worth
more than a padded one.

**Dense.** A year of narrated days. Now the threads are real and the article
earns its sections.

## Length

A lede, and a paragraph or two per thread. A dense year is several hundred
words, not several thousand — and it does not grow without bound as the year
fills. When you add a thread to a long article, ask whether an older one has
become a line rather than a section.

## Linking

Link the days your sections rest on, so a claim can be opened — but only days
listed for you, only by their exact link, and only once each. The same for
people, places and organizations. A section with no day behind it is usually a
section with no evidence behind it.
